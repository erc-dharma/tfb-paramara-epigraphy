Metres: Verses 1-2 Śārdūlavikrīḍita; vv. 3 and 21 Mālinī; vv. 4-12, 16-17, 19, 31, 40 and 45 Upajāti; v.23
	    Indravajrā; vv.13-14, 18, 20, 41-42 and 46 Vasantatilakā; vv.15, and 34-35 Svāgatā;
	    vv.22, 27-30, 36-39 and 44 Anuṣṭhubh, vv.24-25 BHujaṅgaprayāta; v.26 Āryā;
	    vv.32-33 Śālinī; v.43 Mattamayūra.
Gist: A praśati, a laudatory account and records the innovation at a temple of Paṭṭa-Nārāyaṇa by a Brāhmaṇa minister of Pratāpasiṃha,
	  a Paramāra ruler of Chandrāvati. 
Published reference: Trivedi (1978), no. 82.

{1} From the original stone and inked impressions.
{2} Expressed by a symbol.
{3} This daṇḍa, as some others below, looks like a mātrā attached to the preceding letter, and there are
	examples of the reverse case also, all of which are not noted here.
{4} For bhagavato, which would not suit the metre. The flaw may perhaps be eradicated by reading
	devasyādbhutakarmaṇo bhagavato.
{5} Read -jjvala, and also note the spelling of the name of the sage with the palatal in this and in the 
	fourth line.
{6} And hence we get the name paṭṭa Nārāyaṇa. 
{7} The use of cha between the two names is wrong; it is adopted for metrical exigencies.
{8} This daṇḍa is redundant.
{9} The fifth akṣara in this line was at first incised as di and later on corrected by erasing the horizontal
	stroke following it. Similarly, the ninth and tenth letters were originally cut as vīrā and later on corrected.
{10} As also noted by Śāstri, what is stated in this verse is supported by the fact that on the bank of the 
	 tank that at Achalagaḍh, from which place we have the inscription No. 65, there is a statue of Dhārāvarṣa
	 with a bow in his hands and three bufalloes standing before him with a hole running through their bellies.
	 In my visit to the place I also noticed an inscription on the side of the statue but it is highly weather-
	 worn to be made out.
{11} Pradyumna was the son of Kṛṣṇa and is known to have killed the demon Śambara.
{12} The number of the verse is written above the line.
{13} It is an abstract noun from mahīmukhya, meaning mahīpāla, i.e., a Kṣatriya. The daṇḍa that follows
	 was at first engraved as the sign for medial short i and later on the curve above was scored off by a 
	 slanting stroke.
{14} Before ddha the sign for the secondary i was at first engraved and later on the curve above was scored 
	 off.
{15} This word, which was omitted in this line, is engraved in the next line just below where it is to be 
	 supplied, with an arrow mark and double daṇḍa on its either side, to draw attention.
{16} These five curves are redundant. After nē in munē that follows, the sign of visarga was at first en-
	 graved and later on scratched off, leaving the mark.
{17} This daṇḍa looks like a mātrā and the mātrā in the preceding tō looks like a daṇḍa. The five sacri
	 fies referred to in the latter half of this verse are those connected with bhūta, manuṣya, pitṛ, deva and brahman.
{18} Above this letter a rēpha was at first engraved and later on erased, leaving the mark.
{19} The first letter in this word was originally engraved as śre and the sign of mātrā was subsequently
	 erased and the sign of the secondary u was put. 
{20} The two letters giving a name are totally lost in the original.
{21} On the second of these akṣaras a repha was at first engraved and later on erased, leaving the mark.
	 The last letter is deformed.
{22} Originally sa, later on corrected by overwriting.
{23} The space for the mātrā of ā is left blank.
{24} This and the fourth foot of this verse show a wrong pause fall.
{25} Probably to be restored to ityavetya su..
{26} That is, with observances or ceremonies calculated to remove calamities.
{27} This stich means taht 'he removed the order or the belief that a Brāhmaṇa is only to take and not
	 to give'.
{28} The intended reading is perhaps sāvayavasāṃga for sāṃgopāṃga, with the main and sunsidiary. The 
	 horizontal stroke of the preceding m is missing and thus it appears as g.
{29} The word kārmaṇa means witchcraft, for which, cf. Vikramāṅkadevacarita(Bombay), II, 14 and VIII, 2.
{30} This akṣara is blurred but the reading of the preceding one is certain and the one that follows it is
	 rather deformed. Should we take it as -pati dhaṣka in the sense of -dharṣa or dhṛṣṭa, meaning
	 assaulting? Sastri read the word as Turuṣka, but the mātrā of t is clear.
{31} Only the consonant of this akṣara with the following visarga is certaain and all the rest part of it are
	 corrected later on. The anusvāra above, too, is clear and some mātrā originally put above is scratched
	 off. The reading of all these parts is uncertain.
{32} The first letter of the name may also have been Mū. But the consonant is more like S.
{33} In this line, and below too, the signs of anusvāra are only dots, some of which appear to have 
	 been inserted later on.
{34} The same person is mentioned in its sanskrit from Vijayāditya in 1.3 above.
{35} Before this word is engraved another name Sathiradevaḥ, probably for Sārthadevaḥ, which was later
	 on erased.
{36} The preceding word is perhaps to be read as devaḍā and the word dōṇakārī has already explained by
	 Sastri as a combination of drōṇa and khārī, both in the sense of measure. The whole of the remain-
	 ing portion is in the local dialect.
{37} It is a contraction of either Rājputra or Rāūla. The reading of the name of the village that precedes
	 is certain but it is not known whether the first letter is a part of it or to be taken as śrī, an honorific
	 prefix. Both ḍhivaḍā and ḍhīkaḍā (or ḍhīmaḍā) are local words denoting a well.
{38} This daṇḍa is superfluous. Arahaṭṭa (or arghaṭṭa) is often, as taken here by Sastri to be a 'Persian
	 wheel', but I find nothing like Persian in it. It only means, literally, has pots attatched to aras, i.e.,
	 spokes or radius of a wheel (to lift water), as we find used in Rajasthan (for irrigation) even today.
{39} This word too has not been explained before and some misunderstanding about its meaning still prevails,
	 for which see P. B. P., p.234 where is has been taken as a tax on chōllikās brought from outside the
	 town. I take it as a combination of two words, viz. cha (and) ulāpikā which is formed from ulapa
	 (grass). Bundles of grass for cattle fodder are brought from the jungles even to day. D. C. Ganguly
	 read the word as Cōpalikā (H.P.D.,p.324) but did not explain it.
{40} This word means spreading out (articles for sale) before maṇḍapikā, i.e., a market pavilion where they
	 were taxed.
{41} The reading of this line, which is imbedded in the portion of the wall below, has been adopted from 
	 the text in Ind. Ant., Vol. XIV, p.79.
