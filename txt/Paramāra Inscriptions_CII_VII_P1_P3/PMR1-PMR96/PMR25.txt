{1} From the original stone and impressions.
{2} The first two letters lost here may conjecturally be restored as yanmā.
{3} The anusvāra is ornamentally treated, marked as a lotus with petals.
{4} The sign of visarga is visible on the stone, but has not come out in the impressions.
{5} These three akṣaras, which have been conjecturally restored by Sastri, are distinct on the original.
{6} These two letters are lost and the restoration is only conjectural.
{7} In this and the next line the last letters which are in the brackets are thin and in parts, and the restoration
	is certain.
{8} Read rllīnā. A kāka pada sign at the end of the line shows that the word is continued in the next line.
{9} On some of the letters in this line there are signs of abrasions and not of anusvāra as actually taken by
	Sastri. For example, he read an anusvāra on the second akṣara. Here also the line has a kāka pada
	sign in the end it is marked so close to the preceding akṣara as to appear as a mātrā of ā.
{10} For the idea expressed in the third pāda of this verse, compare the well known saying Viṣṇośc hṛdaye
	 śivaḥ. The consonant t before the third letter and the sign of anusvāra on the ninth are engraved faintly
	 and they were consequently not read by Sastri. They are distinct on the stone.
{11} Here also is the sign of kāka pada in the end.
{12} This and the following letters are on the right side where the same line is continued.
{13} Owing to a redundant stroke of the chisel, this akṣara appears as sa. It may also be noted that the sub
	 total is not given after ha.
{14} The number of verses which are not marked here have been taken by us in continuation of those of the 
	 preceding verses.
{15} This akṣara with some others in the lines that follow, are altogether lost and they have been restored
	 from those appearing in the inscription at Dhār which is the counterpart of the present one.
